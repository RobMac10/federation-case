School of Arts, Tenterfield

Thursday, 24 October 1889

The great question which they had to consider was, whether the time had not now arisen for the creation on this Australian continent of an Australian Government, as distinct from a local Government and an Australian Parliament.

Surely what the Americans had done by war, the Australians could bring about in peace.

He believed that the time had come, and if two Governments set an example, the others must soon of necessity follow.

The thing would have to be done, and to put it off would only tend to make the difficulties which stood in the way greater.

Case file

CASE FILE: FEDERATION / 1901

Date of incident   1 January 1901   (Parkes’s Tenterfield speech, 24 October 1889; he died 27 April 1896)

Location   Centennial Park, Sydney · and the six colonies that became the Commonwealth

Status. The new nation began · Who had a say, and who was left out, is still the question

What is agreed · from the 1889–1902 records, and after

Sir Henry Parkes, five times Premier of New South Wales, spoke at a banquet in the School of Arts at Tenterfield on 24 October 1889. He called for an Australian government and an Australian parliament, and for a convention to draw up a constitution. The next morning, The Sydney Morning Herald printed a long report of the speech, mostly in reported speech — “he said,” “they had to consider” — not as a word-for-word script in the first person.

On 6 February 1890, at a banquet in Melbourne for the Australasian Federation Conference, Parkes responded to the toast “A United Australasia.” The line most often remembered from that night is: “The crimson thread of kinship runs through us all.” In the same speech he spoke of a shared British origin. That “us” did not include everyone who lived on this continent.

Parkes died in Sydney on 27 April 1896. He did not see 1 January 1901, when the Commonwealth of Australia came into being at a ceremony in Centennial Park, Sydney. Edmund Barton became the first Prime Minister.

The Constitution had been agreed to in referendums, colony by colony, in 1898, 1899 and 1900, and then passed by the British Parliament. Who could vote in those referendums was not the same in every colony. In most colonies the voters were adult men. Women could already vote in South Australia, and, from 1899, in Western Australia, and they could take part in the later referendums there. Aboriginal people were not treated the same way in every colony. In Western Australia, and in Queensland, most Aboriginal people were kept off the roll. The picture is more complicated in some other colonies, and this file does not pretend it was simple.

The Constitution, as it stood in 1901, said two things that matter for this case. Section 127 said that, in reckoning the numbers of the people, “aboriginal natives shall not be counted.” Section 51(xxvi), the “race power,” gave the Commonwealth Parliament power to make special laws for “the people of any race, other than the aboriginal race in any State.” Both provisions were later changed, in 1967. This file does not treat 1967 as the end of the story.

On 12 June 1902 the Commonwealth Franchise Act became law. It gave the federal vote to men and women who were twenty-one or over, British subjects, resident, and on the roll — and then shut many people out. It said: “No aboriginal native of Australia Asia Africa or the Islands of the Pacific except New Zealand shall be entitled to have his name placed on an Electoral Roll unless so entitled under section forty-one of the Constitution.” Section 41, and how it was later read, is still argued over. In 1901 the new Parliament also passed the Immigration Restriction Act, which used a dictation test to keep many people out.

New Zealand did not join the Commonwealth. Parkes had spoken of “Australasia” at the 1890 banquet; the nation that began in 1901 was the six Australian colonies.

Three witnesses

The same three questions for each: What did they say they saw? · Could they really have seen it? · Why might they say it? Each has a real photograph, in the public domain. No likeness of a real person has been invented.

Edmund Barton

Federation campaigner · First Prime Minister, 1 January 1901 · Died 1920

He helped to carry the federal cause after Parkes died, and he was sworn in as the first Prime Minister at Centennial Park.

Source. The Ashfield gathering of 1897 is the usual source for his best-known line about a nation and a continent. He was there in 1897, four years before the ceremony of 1 January 1901. The photograph is from 1898, National Library of Australia, and is in the public domain in Australia (taken before 1 January 1955). No likeness has been invented.

What they said. At a federation rally in Ashfield, in 1897, he is recorded as saying: “For the first time in history, we have a nation for a continent and a continent for a nation.” That line is often later attached to 1 January 1901. This file keeps it in 1897, where the record places it. In 1901 he became the first Prime Minister of the new Commonwealth.

Could they really have seen it? He was a leading federalist, and he was at the centre of the campaign and of the first government. He could speak about the nation he wanted. He could not, from Ashfield in 1897, have seen the ceremony of 1901, or the Franchise Act of 1902. He also could not speak for people who were given no vote, and no place in the count.

Why might they say it? To persuade people to vote yes, and to describe the kind of nation the campaigns were offering. A phrase about “a nation for a continent” is a claim about land and people. Students can ask who was included in that “we.”

Rose Scott

New South Wales suffragist · Founding president, Women’s Political Educational League, 1902 · Died 1925

She spent years campaigning for women to have a say in the laws that governed them. The new federal vote, when it came, did not include everyone.

Source. Her public work is documented in contemporary records and in later biographical accounts. This card does not invent a speech for her, and it does not put words in quotation marks that this file has not checked word for word. The photograph is a real studio portrait of July 1903, State Library of New South Wales, public domain (taken before 1 January 1955). No likeness has been invented.

What they said. This file does not invent her wording. What the 1902 Act itself said is not in doubt. It gave the federal vote to men and women who were twenty-one or over, British subjects, and on the roll. In the same Act it shut out any “aboriginal native of Australia Asia Africa or the Islands of the Pacific except New Zealand,” unless that person was already entitled under section 41 of the Constitution. Scott became founding president of the Women’s Political Educational League in the year that Act was passed.

Could they really have seen it? She was in New South Wales, and she had long worked for the women’s vote. She could see what the new federal law offered to women who were British subjects on the roll. She was not in every colony, and she was not the voice of the people the Act named in order to shut them out. A later photograph, from July 1903, cannot prove what she said in 1901.

Why might they say it? Suffragists had fought for women to be included. The federal law that followed included many women, and still left many people out. Students can set her campaign beside the words of the Act, and ask whose say the new nation was built on.

Mei Quong Tart

Chinese-Australian merchant and community figure, Sydney · Died 1903

A well-known Sydney merchant, he wrote to the first Prime Minister about how the new Commonwealth’s immigration law was affecting Chinese people.

Source. The National Archives of Australia holds a letter from Quong Tart to Edmund Barton, dated 8 January 1903, about the Immigration Restriction Act 1901 — including domicile certificates, the education test, and state deposits. This card summarises what that archive record is, and does not invent sentences from a letter this file has not transcribed word for word. The photograph is a real portrait of about 1900, National Library of Australia, public domain (taken before 1 January 1955). No likeness has been invented.

What they said. On 8 January 1903, two years after the Commonwealth began, he wrote to Prime Minister Barton about the way the Immigration Restriction Act was being used against Chinese people. The Archives describe the letter as dealing with domicile certificates, the education test, and state deposits. The education test — the dictation test — was the device the 1901 Act used to keep many people out, without always naming a race in the test itself.

Could they really have seen it? He lived in Sydney, and he had standing in both Chinese and European circles. He could describe what the new law was doing to people he knew. He wrote after 1901, not at the Centennial Park ceremony, and not as a voter who had been invited to help write the Constitution. He also cannot be made to stand in for Aboriginal people, whose exclusion was written into other clauses.

Why might they say it? To ask the first Prime Minister to look at how the new nation’s law was falling on Chinese residents and travellers. A letter to Barton is evidence of a protest, and of who had to ask. It is not the same thing as having had a say in 1899 or 1900, when the Constitution was put to the referendums.

Why it still matters

The speeches still tell us who was meant by “us all.” The Constitution, and the Franchise Act that followed, still show who was counted, who could vote, and who was left out. Section 127, and the wording of the race power, were later changed, in 1967. The franchise later widened. Those changes matter, and they do not close the file. The question of who gets a say in the nation is not only a question about 1901.

Super Sleuths

Go further only if you want to. Amber marks what is still disputed, or what later writers added. This file is a draft, pending consultation, wherever it touches First Nations histories.

1. The Herald’s Parkes, not a rewritten “I”

The typographic page follows The Sydney Morning Herald of 25 October 1889, p. 8. The speech was given on 24 October. The Herald mostly used reported speech: “the great question which they had to consider was…” Later retellings, including some museum pages, turn this into first person (“we have to consider”). This file does not make that change.

2. “The crimson thread of kinship”

This line is from the Melbourne banquet of 6 February 1890, not from Tenterfield. In the same speech Parkes spoke of a shared British origin. “Us all” was a claim about kinship. It was not a claim that every person on this continent was included. Aboriginal people, and many Chinese-Australians, were outside that “thread” as Parkes drew it.

3. He never saw it happen

Tenterfield, 24 October 1889. Melbourne banquet, 6 February 1890. Parkes died on 27 April 1896. The Commonwealth began on 1 January 1901. The handover line in this file is not a quotation from Parkes. It is this file’s plain statement of those dates.

4. Who could vote in the referendums

The Constitution was put to voters in 1898, 1899 and 1900. The rules were not the same in every colony. Women could vote in South Australia, and, from 1899, in Western Australia. In most other colonies the voters were adult men. Aboriginal people were widely excluded in Queensland and Western Australia. In some other colonies the picture is more complicated, and summaries sometimes flatten it. Western Australia voted in 1900. New Zealand did not join.

5. Section 127, and the race power

Section 127 of the Constitution, as it stood in 1901, said that in reckoning the numbers of the people, “aboriginal natives shall not be counted.” Section 51(xxvi) gave the Commonwealth power to make special laws for “the people of any race, other than the aboriginal race in any State.” Both were changed in 1967. This file does not treat those later changes as if they cancelled the 1901 record, or as if they ended every question about who counts.

6. The 1902 Franchise Act, and section 41

The Commonwealth Franchise Act 1902, assented to on 12 June, gave the federal vote to men and women who met its tests — and then said: “No aboriginal native of Australia Asia Africa or the Islands of the Pacific except New Zealand shall be entitled to have his name placed on an Electoral Roll unless so entitled under section forty-one of the Constitution.” Section 41 is a difficult clause. Later governments and courts often read it narrowly. This file flags that dispute rather than settling it.

7. Quong Tart’s letter, and the dictation test

The Immigration Restriction Act 1901 used a dictation test to keep many people out. On 8 January 1903 Quong Tart wrote to Barton about how that law was affecting Chinese people. The National Archives of Australia describes the letter; this file has not printed a word-for-word extract, because it has not checked a full transcription against the original in this build. In 1894, Sir Henry Parkes had written a letter of introduction for Quong Tart, praising his “energy and integrity.”

8. Barton’s line, and the day it was not said

“For the first time in history, we have a nation for a continent and a continent for a nation” is recorded as Barton’s line at Ashfield in 1897. It is often later told as if it were said at Centennial Park on 1 January 1901. This file keeps the 1897 date. A famous phrase can still be the wrong proof for a day.
